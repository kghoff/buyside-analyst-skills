# Filings research framework checklist

Working rules for reading a company from its own documents. Each is a habit of buy-side practice; extend this file with your own.

## The goal
Distill down to three key drivers. They can be part of the five core value drivers (Part 2), or company- / industry-specific metrics
(ARPU for a subscription business, RPO for SaaS). A subscription streamer, for example: (1) subscriber growth, which may be slowing; (2) ARPU;
(3) margin. Then: how to model each driver, from data the company discloses.

(ARPU = average revenue per user. RPO = remaining performance obligations, contracted revenue not yet recognized. SaaS = software as a service. The five core value drivers: see Part 2.)

Common abbreviations used below: yoy = year over year; qoq = quarter over quarter; bps = basis points (0.01%); FCF = free cash flow; EPS = earnings per share; EBITDA = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; SG&A = selling, general and administrative; COGS = cost of goods sold; P/E = price-to-earnings multiple; LSD / MSD / HSD / LDD = low-single / mid-single / high-single / low-double digit growth.

## Part 1: What readers hunt for in company documents

### Revenue drivers
- 10-K Business section: how the company makes money, customer count, geographies, stated targets (e.g. operating-margin target), partnerships and supplier linkages. Highlight it and keep notes.
- Capture every operating statistic disclosed, even odd bespoke ones (supercharger count; users, trips, bookings, deliveries, paid memberships, ARPU). Capture everything first; distill to key drivers later.
- Press releases often disclose metrics missing from the 10-Q, especially adjusted figures, because the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) scrutinizes filings more closely. Read them for extra operating data.
- Split yoy revenue growth into reported, constant currency, FX (foreign exchange), M&A (mergers and acquisitions), organic, price/mix and volume, wherever disclosed.
- Break revenue apart as far as disclosed: volume, price, organic vs M&A, geography, product, segment. ASP (average selling price) and volume mix predict better than one total-revenue line.
- Segment financials: which segments are shrinking and which carry the profit. On a re-segmentation, record old and new reporting side by side and check the IR (investor relations) site for recasts of 4–12 past quarters.
- The company's top 3–5 earnings drivers, the single key metric it keeps stressing, and what it says it tracks internally. These become what to track.
- Call Q&A shows the current debate. The first three analyst questions point at the key drivers.
- Any new contract, product or program adding revenue the market may not expect: capture its revenue size and incremental margin.

### Revenue quality
- Contract terms in revenue disclosure: term, escalators, lags, unit-driven vs price-driven, recurring vs one-time. Price drives short-term margins; units are the better long-term driver.
- Classify the revenue basket: recurring or SaaS, razor and razor blade, capital or replacement cycle, one-time sale, or habitual pattern.
- Record large one-time contracts in dollars and percent so they do not distort the base and trend.

### Seasonality and comps
- Compute each quarter's historical share of annual revenue, EBITDA and EPS. Many companies give no quarterly guidance, so seasonality is the start. A steep back-half profit ramp raises earnings-cut risk.
- Compute 2-year and 3-year stacked growth (sum of consecutive yoy rates) to see run-rate growth past unusual year-ago comps; cross-check qoq growth against historical seasonality. ALGN's stack showed a slowdown its 1-year growth hid.
- Strip one-time items out of the year-ago quarter for a clean margin comparison, and note them.

### Cost structure and unit economics
- Find the granular cost breakdown in the 10-K. List every expense line and its driver; classify each as fixed, semi-fixed or variable; find the driver of semi-fixed ones (new stores, other expansion metric).
- Paragraphs defining what sits in revenue, COGS, opex, SG&A and EBITDA. SG&A grows with revenue in a growing business, then becomes fixed at maturity.
- Fixed commitments (e.g. content commitments). If volume falls, a fixed cost base can crush margins and management cannot easily respond. Know this before a bear case.
- Unit economics where disclosed (EBITDA per hospital, revenue per store, SG&A per employee). Pick the most granular unit of demand to divide by. Trends can show pressure others miss, such as wage spikes.
- How management has handled margin upside: does it flow to profit, or get reinvested (e.g. R&D when gross margin is strong)? Forecast real behavior.

### Cash, capital intensity and working capital
- Extract the cash-flow cycle: working capital, capex, NOLs (net operating losses, tax shields).
- Compare the cash flow statement with the P&L: where FCF deviates from earnings, such as cash content spend above amortization.
- Net debt, debt maturity schedule, covenants, EBITDA, interest expense and coverage, cash balance. Does the business need capital markets? For unprofitable companies, the liquidity runway and dilution risk.
- Tax-rate guidance, average interest rate on debt and buyback pace, for below-the-line items.

### Capital deployment
- How management actually deploys cash (buyback or M&A), so the model reflects real behavior.
- History across the five options: internal investment, reducing net debt, dividend, buyback and acquisition. Judge each by return to equity holders.
- Prospectuses (S-1, S-3, S-4, S-8): use of proceeds and business details. Use the S-1 for new companies.

### Guidance and credibility
- Inventory guidance and tag each piece hard (press-release metrics) or soft (call commentary, investor presentations, roadshows). Date the last update. Soft guidance deserves close attention.
- Long-term goals (often 3-year targets for growth, gross margin, EBITDA margin): compare with annual guidance and with what was delivered.
- Guidance cadence: the Q3 call usually gives next-year headwinds and tailwinds; Q4 gives formal guidance. Check whether guidance needs a back-half "hockey stick".
- Management's historical beat versus initial guidance (a typical cushion is 5–7%) and its propensity to miss.
- Use historical incremental margins as a lie detector on 3–5 year margin guidance. Incremental margin above current margin implies rising margins.
- Earnings transcripts: read at least the last 4, and 8–12 for a deep dive, for key drivers and the assumptions embedded in guidance. Improving momentum against a low guide is the classic beat-and-raise setup.
- GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles) to non-GAAP reconciliation history (amortization, acquisition costs, restructuring), so both bases can be modeled.
- Critical accounting estimates and GAAP / non-GAAP policies. Could a discretionary policy, such as an amortization schedule, be gamed?
- SEC correspondence letters: an early warning of a restatement. Most people never read them.

### Management and strategy over time
- 10-K MD&A (Management's Discussion and Analysis) is a must-read: it shows how management thinks about and reacts to a changing business over time. Only about 15–20% of a 10-K matters.
- Communication baseline: read 6–12 prior transcripts and decks, note key language, tone and framing and recurring spin (a margin hit called an "investment"), so later deviations stand out.
- Subtle shifts in management tone on one issue across calls: early signs of change not yet in the price.
- Management comments are marketing. Note them but discount them.
- Capture management commentary on each line item (weak demand with a new product coming, a competitor launch) next to the assumption it affects.
- Conference transcripts are more valuable than the decks; executives present between quarters.
- Proxy (DEF 14A): skim for executive compensation, long-term targets and the incentives they create.
- 8-Ks and press releases: usually unimportant, but a bad one matters (e.g. an officer change). Watch for unscheduled material events.
- 10-Qs are mainly for pulling quarterly financial statements, not reading front to back.

### Competition and industry structure
- Industry facts the company cites in 10-Ks, decks, investor days and calls: operating conditions, industry data, reputable industry forecasts. This is the company's own picture of its industry.
- Investor days: promotional, so read skeptically, but good for long-term vision and learning the industry. Read several together; themes repeated across events build real expertise.
- Industry growth drivers, competitive dynamics, technology, secular and cyclical trends, supply chain, capital cycles, cultural and consumer shifts (retail vs e-commerce vs B2B), as the company describes them.
- Past failure causes the company or peers admit (missed product cycle, underspent R&D, overspent G&A, bad M&A, too much debt): the industry's potholes and risk externalities.
- Industry data series the company cites (e.g. trade-association shipment data) and whether its own growth tracks that series.
- Peers, industry bodies and conferences the company names: leads for the competitor set and for sources operators read.
- Customer, segment and unit disclosures (same-store sales, units, franchise mix, margin, ROIC) needed to build the company's slice of an industry P&L.
- Disclosed partnerships and supply-chain linkages, as read-throughs to other companies.

### Industry map
- Industry map: where the company sits in the value chain, who sells to whom, who pays, and who sits between it and the end customer. Draw it before analyzing the company. The filings research draws it from the company's own descriptions (report section 02).

### Regulation and who pays
- Risk factors are mostly legal boilerplate. Skim for new litigation or a newly added risk.

## Part 2: Lenses for the report

### Business quality
- Three lenses: business model (how $1 flows through the P&L, customer relationship, cost structure, cash economics), business quality, and business momentum. Use all three to frame the whole report.
- Three foundational questions: how does the business make money, is it a good or bad business, and how does cash flow through it (the cash cycle)?
- Good vs bad business: good ones avoid catastrophe and are stable; quality is a FCF stream. Check organic growth, ROIC, FCF margin, ROIC above WACC, plus emotional customer relationship, special sauce, criticality.
- Four quality checks: criticality (would anyone care if it vanished), self-funding (could you own it if the market closed for 5 years), externality (does it survive a recession or a stroke of a pen), moat (could you compete with it given $10bn).
- Moat and terminal value: most value sits in the terminal value, so an eroding moat threatens it. A moat keeps ROIC above WACC.
- ROIC (return on invested capital) = NOPAT (net operating profit after tax) / invested capital. It captures profitability and capital intensity and predicts cash production; capital-intensive businesses have lower ROIC despite better margins. Pair it with organic growth and durability.
- Margin level alone doesn't matter; cash production and ROIC above WACC (weighted average cost of capital) do. A 1.5%-margin distributor at 21% ROIC can beat a 35%-margin business at 7.4%.
- Business viability test: three ways to break it are falling revenue, falling margin and soaring build-out costs. Each cuts ROIC and ROE (return on equity).
- FCF conversion (FCF / adjusted net income) is an under-appreciated quality test. Cash doesn't lie; adjusted metrics can hide the ball.
- Normalizing FCF: FCF bounces with capex cycles, working capital, NOLs and margin. Set normal revenue, cash margin and capex to get normal FCF per share and yield. Don't cut value for one bad year, any more than a farm is worth less after one bad harvest.
- Margin on cash can look very different from reported margin: a streamer's cash operating margin can sit far below its reported margin.
- Statement roles: the P&L supplies the forecast metrics; the balance sheet is risk to equity (leverage, liquidity, calls on cash); the cash flow statement shows normalized FCF.
- Before reacting to a leverage spike, check it is not just depressed EBITDA: look at debt maturities and any need to raise equity.
- Assessing management: needed to understand the business, judge the wisdom of initiatives and spot inflections. Management meets investors because the stock chart is their report card.

### Value drivers
- Valuation rests on four forecast metrics: revenue, EBITDA, EPS and FCF.
- The five things to focus on: organic revenue growth, margin trajectory, capital intensity, capital deployment, terminal value perception. All value creation reduces to these five, so deep dive around them.
- "Pull one driver, hold all else constant" test: in a 30-year DCF (discounted cash flow), change one of the five core drivers and watch fair P/E (for example, 10% growth for 10 years versus none can roughly double the fair multiple). The biggest mover is a key driver.
- Revenue is generally the most important line. Competitive industries settle near 10–15% ROIC, and a 10% revenue shortfall can turn a 15% return into a loss because costs do not shrink.
- Terminal value perception: about two-thirds of value sits beyond year 10, so a shaky long-term view moves the multiple. Zeroing it took a 17.1x P/E to 4.7x.
- Declining businesses have little or negative terminal value (permanent FCF burn), so a low P/E can be a value trap.
- Binary terminal values: when the market doubts or believes the long-term outcome, the multiple swings widely (SNAP 3x to 25x revenue). Stable staples hold steadier P/E channels.
- Fixed vs variable costs: more fixed cost means higher incremental and decremental margins. Fixed-cost leverage is the usual source of above-base incrementals. Operating leverage cuts both ways; neither is good or bad alone.
- Variable costs rarely move margin, except through price-cost dynamics (price increases versus input-cost inflation).
- Price increases help margins more than volume: a price increase drops nearly all to profit; volume brings its own variable costs.
- Mix affects margin, not just growth: if faster-growing segments carry lower margins, total margin can fall as revenue accelerates.
- Incremental margin = yoy profit change / yoy revenue change. Margins are coincident, incrementals leading: above-base incrementals mean base margin rises (Mastercard 13% to 53% on 55% incrementals).
- Unit economics: divide aggregate metrics by the simplest, most granular unit of demand and ask which levers improve per-unit economics. Critical for unprofitable companies.
- Show unit economics on both an accrual and a cash basis; aggregate lines can mask the true economics.
- Unit economics are a trajectory, not a static number: Mastercard cut marketing from 32c to 5c per revenue dollar and margins soared.
- Play the "what if" game on per-unit lines to see what a good future state looks like and which line drives it.
- Current-state economics: current and normal margin, current and normal FCF margin, growth and its acceleration, whether capital intensity is rising. Often already baked into the stock.
- Current vs future state: loss-making isn't automatically bad but raises risk. Value the move from weak to strong economics: TSLA gross profit per delivery rose 34% and the stock 33x.
- Value is highly sensitive to the reinvestment rate: $100 FCF reinvested at 20% vs 5% was worth $17.28 vs $11.58 a share. Returning cash is the safest default.
- Buybacks are like buying out a partner: remaining owners increase their pro-rata share of income.

### Industry
- Dimensions of expertise: frameworks and tools; industry (growth drivers, competitive dynamics, technology, secular/cyclical trends, supply chains, capital cycles); company (model, quality, strategy, management, key drivers, product quality). Cover all three.
- Analyzing an industry: note economic and cultural trends and industry supply/demand, then show how the company fits. "The best sailor cannot sail without wind"; the industry trend can be nearly deterministic.
- Industry P&L: combine top public players' statements into one industry P&L, BS, CF; track revenue, margins, ROIC, key metrics, P/E. It shows peak, trough or mid-cycle, returns vs cost of capital, and new capex cycles. The filings research doesn't build it: the later /model-industry skill builds it as a model tab from the filings and street research reports. The filings research records only the company's own slice (Part 1).
- Industry P&L rule: chart each metric over time (comps, unit growth, margin, ROIC, franchise mix, P/E premium), ask why each moved, and whether the move was durable or a one-time share grab.
- Industry P&L rule: separate unit growth in mature vs growing geographies, and ask how much margin or ROIC tailwind (e.g. re-franchising) is left.
- Industry P&L rule: treat a historical acceleration as possibly a cycle, not the norm, then ask which outcome prevails in the next 3–5 years.
- Revisit transcripts (and sell-side notes, in the street research) from periods when industry numbers moved sharply, to learn what was being said then.

### Key drivers and debates
- Distill ETIK (Everything There Is to Know: filings, modeling, public statements, management, channel diligence, experts, alternative data) to three key drivers. A model may have 800 rows; differentiation is built on the three.
- Find key drivers three ways: academic value drivers, model sensitivities (what moves value, which segments matter), and narrative analysis (the debate, what the sell-side asks, first call questions).
- Frame key drivers as questions ("How will this company fare against its largest competitor?"). Categories: numbers, valuation, terminal value, algorithm, growth durability, regulatory.
- Flag each key driver as researchable by channel checks or forecastable with data, so follow-up refines it.
- Core-debate types: market sizing, structural, cyclical and pricing trajectory, competitive dynamics, moat, unit economics, cash economics, margins vs peers, corporate initiatives, growth algorithm, regulation, FX, valuation, capital deployment, accounting, quality, management.
- Core debate chain: the debate (what moves the stock, bull/bear tension), the consensus view embedded in price, tailored analytics, then the thesis (how an expectations gap closes).
- Key-driver insight: distill the decision into bear, base and bull scenarios on the make-or-break drivers, and split the return into EPS and multiple.
- Insight dashboard: per key driver, track profit sources by segment, data correlations, channel checks and read-throughs. "Improving/deteriorating" can be enough when precision isn't possible.
- Tyranny of the anecdote: one data point is likely noise (ten channel checks averaged -2.7% though the first said +8%). Weigh many data points; never extrapolate from one.
- Weight of evidence: list confirming and disconfirming nuggets and weigh them dispassionately.

### Stock
- Vectors of understanding: economy, industry, business and stock. Industry sets the trade winds; the business's key drivers should also drive the stock.
- Stock vs business: right on the business but wrong on the stock is still wrong. Over longer durations the stock reflects the business.
- Business momentum: markets reward acceleration and punish deceleration. Use 1-, 2- and 3-year growth rows; track growth, margin and capital-intensity direction; ask if drivers are structural or ephemeral.
- Direction of fundamentals (improving or deteriorating) generally drives the stock's direction. Track net adds, growth, margin change and incrementals together.
- What moves the stock: identify which KPIs (key performance indicators) influence valuation, then anticipate them. Example: the multiple tracks the key driver's level (low comps, low P/E).
- If key drivers are unclear, ask what historically drove large price moves; that is a key driver or strongly related to one.
- Baselining and inflection: the current state is mostly priced in; prices reflect reality ~90% of the time. Research hunts the 10%: deviation from the embedded view not yet priced.
- Catalysts: ruthlessly list the company's own (launches, contract wins, regulatory events, earnings dates, investor days) with PSUC (probability of success), upside and downside. An unconsidered catalyst is not tolerable.
- Incrementalism: for a genuinely new change, incremental revenue × incremental margin, less tax, over diluted shares, added to EPS at the base multiple, gives a first-pass price move.
- Revisions drive price: at a constant P/E, a 50% EPS cut means a 50% price fall. Large variance between our EPS and consensus may flag an interesting idea.
- Differentiation: state where we differ from consensus and why, with a variance table and the bull/bear tension. The E in P/E matters because estimates vary widely.
- Structurally HSD/LDD growers generally trade at higher P/Es than MSD growers, so segment mix shifts matter to valuation.
- What the PM (portfolio manager) wants: size and liquidity, risk/reward, growth trajectory, margin trajectory, EPS algorithm, variance versus Street, valuation.

## Part 3: Forecast-structure guidance

### Revenue frameworks
- Growth stack: stack population, real GDP, inflation and share-of-wallet growth (industry growth beyond nominal GDP) to reach industry growth; pair with market share for company growth. Fits consumption-linked markets; can be built bottom-up (users × share × price).
- Mix accelerator: project each segment at its own rate; total growth accelerates or decelerates as mix shifts, even with unchanged segment rates. Fits a fast segment gaining weight (China/EM (emerging markets), robotics, biologics).
- Comp waterfall: new stores ramp over years (e.g. 25/50/75/100% of mature), lifting same-store sales while maturing; comps fade when openings stop. Fits restaurants and retail.
- Box replicator: model unit economics per box, then the number of boxes, and whether economics hold under cannibalization. Fits store, hospital or gym chains with repeatable units.
- Ramp curve: average launch curves of analogous products (the company's earlier drugs; Facebook; Tesla for Rivian) as the starting curve. Fits drugs, EVs, social media.
- Creative lags: use a leading demographic series shifted by a known lag (SCI's funeral demand from births ~85 years earlier). Fits demand set by a predictable, lagged population trend.
- Mix extrapolation: when two segments pull opposite ways, project revenue by segment and apply each segment's margin (HCA payers; SCI cremation vs burial); find an analogue for the mix. Fits mixes with differing growth and profit.
- Mid-cycle / historical average: historical growth average and mean reversion. Simple and powerful, but know the drivers and how mix evolved; consensus sometimes uses it, so understanding drivers can reveal a variant view.
- Data correlations: correlate outside data (e.g. Google Trends) with company growth, quarterize it, test supply-chain lags (a 2-quarter lag gave 87% for RVs). Fits where outside data leads reported revenue; the data comes in the street research.
- Approach revenue from several angles (geography, volume × price, product, market share) and use the most predictive; detailed builds beat a single line. Mind comps, seasonality and durability.
- Always put total market revenue in the model, whichever revenue method you use.
- Keep forward organic growth within a 5-year-average mean-reversion band.
- Unit-economics forecast: per-unit lines (take rate, COGS, S&M (sales and marketing), other opex) × unit volume. Only where units and cost lines are disclosed; otherwise choose another framework.
- Subscription-style key-driver grid: set high, current and low five-year member growth, ARPU and margin against price.
- Segment forecasts matter where the business has different engines: crack open revenue and profit to see the core profit drivers.

### Costs and margins
- Cost method: variable expenses as a percent of sales or cost per unit; fixed costs at a simple growth rate; for mixed costs, find the driver.
- bps yoy vs incremental margins: model constant incremental margin on new revenue, not constant bps expansion. Constant bps quietly implies ever-higher incrementals (31% rising to 40%); constant incrementals give tapering bps.
- Mostly-fixed cost base: run revenue through high incremental margins up and high decremental margins down. NFLX at 50% incrementals and 50% decrementals gave wide value bars.
- "Model to reality": trace how a dollar flows through the P&L and how the CFO would respond to a revenue drop, such as cutting marketing.
- Model the cash economics, not just the P&L, when cash spend differs from accounting expense (content, capex, working capital).
- Below the line: forecast the net debt stack and average interest rate; use the guided tax rate; share count from buyback dollars / share price.
- Model capital deployment (buybacks, bolt-on M&A) instead of letting cash pile up on the balance sheet.
- GAAP vs non-GAAP: ideally model both. Estimates are compared with consensus, so headline whichever basis consensus uses.

### Durability of growth
- What drives growth: share gains in a slow market, or price? Is the stock priced for HSD/LDD growth? Can competitors stop the share bleeding?
- Share gains end abruptly; growth then snaps to the market rate. Check how long share gains can last.
- Pricing-driven growth is generally less durable than volume-driven growth. Check whether a pricing cycle is near its cap.
- Express growth in dollars as well as percent and sanity-check the incremental dollars each year needs. A slowing rate on a huge base still needs enormous revenue; yesterday's compounder likely isn't tomorrow's, so model deceleration.
- Durability is critical for high-valuation stocks: companies have life cycles, markets extrapolate, and larger bases make high growth harder.
- Base rates: sustained 10%+ growth is rare (McKinsey: ~25% of companies shrank, 21% grew above 10%); about 4% of companies created all net wealth.
- Classify the ramp: quick-ramp (tech) or durable-ramp box replicator (WMT). For restaurants, penetration near 50% of TAM (total addressable market) is a short trigger.
- P/E multiples tend to track revenue growth; when growth slows, P/Es crack. Work out the 10+ year runway and the secular driver.
- The market loves a small share of TAM with an easy path to taking share (e-commerce, online ads, cloud).
- Capital-cycle revenue can fall for years while the underlying business grows 10% a year. Watch for an air pocket in cyclical revenue.
- Compounders vs melting ice cubes: structural, durable growth is chronically underpriced and structural decline overpriced. Say which the company is.
- Compounder test: long growth runway, defensible business model, 15%+ EPS algorithm. A slightly expensive-looking compounder may still be undervalued if the runway is believed.
- Melting ice cube check: with zero terminal value, the right multiple is far below where it trades.
- Structural short signs: a secular threat plus a fixed footprint, disintermediation, or an adoption cycle that has run its course.

### Process
- Collect everything first: 10 years of 10-Ks, 10-Qs, call and investor-event transcripts, decks; a decade or more gives historical context. Batching up front makes the model and analysis far more efficient.
- Reading stack: 10-K, transcripts, conference and investor-day material, proxies (sell-side comes in the street research). For each document, notate only the 5–15% likely to shape the thesis.
- Keep a running dated file per company: notes and a distillation of each filing, call and deck, plus a running question list. Review the model before each year's reading.
- The model is the process backbone: understand the business, assess health (growth, margin, capital intensity), read momentum, value independently, find revision opportunities, contextualize news. Top firms mandate bespoke builds for data ownership.
- Choose the three key drivers first, then deep-dive them for differentiation and build sensitivities. Within 4–5 days: top 3–5 earnings drivers, market-embedded expectations, base/bull/bear. Basic model and reverse DCF before the detailed model.
- First cut: ignore consensus; build a naive baseline at historical trend growth and margin change (e.g. 4–6% organic, 25–50 bps expansion).
- Check whether conditions run above or below trend. At peak or trough, bias toward mean reversion; be wary of hockey-stick forecasts.
- Compare guidance assumptions with your own research: aggressive, conservative or appropriate? Name specifically what differs.
- Detailed forecast: split organic growth into components, margin into cost growth and incremental margin, and cash flow into the cash cycle and real-world cash use.
- On key debates, take a probabilistic view and reflect it in the model.
- Reverse DCF: input growth, margin, ROIC and discount-rate profile to see what multiple it supports (long compounding supports very high multiples); solve for price, then titrate the three key drivers. "Finger in the air", not scientific. Not part of the filings research: valuation is the later /model-dcf skill.
- Discount rate, as an example: CAPM (capital asset pricing model) cost of equity = risk-free + beta × equity risk premium; with no debt, WACC equals it. Not part of the filings research: valuation is the later /model-dcf skill.
- Imputing a 10% return from a future price and EPS gives the "fair" starting P/E.
- Valuation scenarios: a 3-year target, a 12-month base target and a 12-month risk case, each from EPS and a target P/E. Not part of the filings research: valuation is the later /model-dcf skill.
- Keep the model in sections (data inputs, operating data, segments, P&L, balance sheet, cash flow) with comments, guidance rows, historic seasonality rows and consensus rows (from the street research) under the forecast.
- Keep a key-driver research and monitoring plan (catalysts, read-throughs, adaptive risk/reward, thesis-creep prevention, inside vs outside view); update forecasts when data shows a driver inflecting.

## Part 4: Needs outside sources (the street research)
- Consensus estimates: compare our forecast with the average, the high-low EPS range and revisions, in a variance table. P/E depends on which "E" you use.
- Consensus trend and revision charts show which direction estimates are moving.
- Sell-side estimates cluster around guidance (weak incentive for accuracy), so expect consensus-hugging and look for variance.
- Upside in earnings revisions: which business drivers are improving and not yet captured by sell-side estimates?
- Two sell-side initiations, key upgrade/downgrade notes and industry primers (often 200+ pages). Sell-side is strong on industry knowledge, weak on stock picking and model accuracy, positively biased, and sets consensus.
- Sell-side "Sector 101" meetings and teach-ins: read their primers first, ignore buy/sell calls at this stage.
- Sell-side outlooks (usually December or January) to check all industry themes, and topical deep dives on hot themes, taken with a grain of salt.
- Research aggregators and systems (AlphaSense, Tegus, Thomson Eikon, AlphaTheory) and note types (earnings, NDR (non-deal roadshow), survey).
- Street narrative: sell-side notes, other buy-siders, specialist sales, trader flow, blogs, short-seller research, and how the stock traded around past news and seasons.
- Channel checks, surveys and expert networks (GLG, Guidepoint, Coleman; transcript libraries Tegus, AlphaSense, Third Bridge) for key-driver diligence, with many contacts, not one. Track the one key metric incessantly.
- Corporate access: calls with management, IR, the CFO, competitors and channel contacts; investor conferences; non-deal roadshows. IR helps with interest, tax rate and share-count assumptions.
- Industry, government and economic data (BLS, FOIA requests, industry data providers and their historical reports): get raw data, quarterize it and correlate it where relevant.
- Industry and trade-association forecasts: a decent starting point for market growth, but often trend extrapolation and survey work.
- Industry sector growth, market size and share data for the market-sizing debate.
- Alternative and web data: Google Trends, web scraping, web traffic, executive monitors, transactions, receipts, apps, foot traffic, jobs, import/export, scanner data; correlate with company results, with a supply-chain lag.
- Analogue companies for ramp curves, mix shifts and valuation comps (Facebook, Tesla, UK cremation rate, close-peer P/E).
- Long-run demographic and macro series for growth stacks and creative lags (population, GDP, births).
- Empirical corporate growth data (McKinsey) for base rates on how rarely companies sustain 10%+ growth.
- Peer margins and multiples: rank against ~30 peers on gross margin, SG&A, R&D, operating margin; a competitor's margin shows the gap (NVST 14.9% vs XRAY 19.5%); does gap closure justify a P/E premium?
- Connect the dots: earlier-reporting peers' results as read-throughs to the next reporter, using side-by-side comp tables.
- Learn an industry bottom-up, player by player; each sub-player teaches a segment (one name for Medicare Advantage, another for Medicaid).
- Roadmap for learning an industry, 10 steps: primers, Sector 101, trade magazines, consultants, leading companies, past failures, industry aggregate P&L/BS/CF, non-sell-side conferences, sell-side conferences, build a network.
- Trade magazines and newsletters operators read, plus podcasts and Substack.
- Sector textbooks for complicated concepts, plus books and biographies on the industry and its founders.
- Industry consultants, former CEOs and private investors for education and historical context.
- Industry panels (sell-side, news organizations, universities, YouTube) and sector conferences, both operator and sell-side.
- Investment memos, hedge fund letters, and podcast and TV interviews of the industry, for historical context.
- Product field research and in-person diligence (trade shows, factory visits).
- Historical valuation multiples and price history; compare current NTM (next twelve months) P/E with the stock's long-term average.
- Name tracker (price performance, valuation, revisions, catalysts, earnings dates, internal targets) and a sector comp sheet ranked on a valuation multiple.
- Weekly or monthly catalyst watch, including non-earnings events like regulatory announcements and monthly industry data.
- Industry map tools (e.g. the advertising technology landscape) to find whom to talk to.
- Historical sector return tables: base-rate returns, drivers of large industry moves, batting average and Sharpe by sector (sectors differ widely in how often they beat the market).
- Winner/loser post-mortems: the top 10 winners and losers each quarter, diagnosing what drove them.
- Case study library: find the model in past situations (turnarounds, spins, re-franchising, SaaS conversions) and apply it to new ones.

## Lessons from a DXCM test run
- Guide-vs-actual: collect every initial guide and every revision with its date, not just the final number. Tag each hard (press release) or soft (call or deck).
- Watch for basis changes (GAAP vs non-GAAP, organic vs reported). DXCM's 2020 margin guide changed basis and had to be excluded from the guide-vs-actual comparison.
- Event decks are near-duplicates (e.g. JPM January vs August): note which slides changed and skip the rest.
- Disclosure coverage drives the model recommendation. Build a disclosure coverage matrix (metric × years available); DXCM's consistent US/International split made the build geography-first, with segment TAM and penetration cross-checks.
- Note what management says about seasonal patterns (e.g. Q1 and Q4) and compute 2-year stacks.
- Surface contradictions between sources rather than resolving them silently; explain the mechanisms and flag them for the street research (DXCM: 10-K Medicare cut vs deck "doubles access", price vs coverage breadth).
