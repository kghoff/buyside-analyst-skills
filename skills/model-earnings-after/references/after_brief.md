# After-print brief (chapter 13, sections 13.6 and 13.7, distilled)

Read this before the assignment. Code has done the arithmetic (surprise, sigmas, drift, IRR); you make the judgments.

## 1. Read the print with SFOT (13.6, "Print Day: The SFOT Framework")
Four questions frame every print. Answer each positive, neutral or negative, with the line that shows it.
- **Surprise:** was the print in line with what the market expected, or a real surprise? A surprise is one that
  forces the market to ratchet its expectations; that is what makes the big moves. A beat the Street already
  expected is not a surprise.
- **Fundamentals:** did business momentum speed up or slow down on the key drivers? Markets extrapolate momentum.
- **Outlook:** did guidance change (raise, hold, cut, initiate, withdraw)?
- **Tone:** did management's language shift against last quarter? Compare with the previous Call Notes columns
  in context.md, not with your memory.

## 2. Was the move signal or noise? (13.6, "Signal or Noise?")
Inside two to three daily sigmas may be noise; far outside, something happened: find it in near-term EPS or in
long-term cash flow.

## 3. The reaction note and the target (13.6, "The Earnings Reaction Note"; "Adjusting the Price Target")
The target moves through the core formula, price = multiple x EPS: what changed EPS (guidance,
driver commentary) and what changed the multiple (momentum, growth, margins, visibility, tone). If the target rose
more than the stock, that is an **under-reaction**: "respectfully, the fund should not sell the stock; it should
consider buying more."

## 4. The thesis checkpoint (13.6, "Earnings as a Checkpoint")
Earnings are a checkpoint on the long-term thesis: are growth, margins and cash flow on track, and is the
expectations gap closing? Answer green, yellow or red. Many checkpoints are yellow. Stay open-minded about whether
the thesis is still on track; do not defend it by habit.

## 5. Summarize the call by topic (13.7, "Summarize the Call")
Take near-verbatim notes on the points that matter, filed by topic so they sit beside last quarter's and show how
the commentary moved. Quote the transcript's own words on the line you cite; never paraphrase inside a quote. A
topic the call never touched gets an empty list, not filler. Q&A themes are the questions analysts pressed on.

## 6. Long-term targets (13.7, "Tracking Long-Term Guidance")
If the company has a long-term target, name it so code can test the pace: a ratable path from today's level to the
target. If reaching it needs a hockey stick that has not appeared, the target is aggressive, and aggressive
long-term guides are often reset on earnings or at investor days.

## 7. The wrap (13.7, "The Wrap and the Go-Forward")
The wrap covers seven things: thoughts on the quarter; how the estimates and the multiple changed; the catalyst
path (next conference, data point, investor day); controversies for management to clarify; updated guidance; the
updated IRR; and the updated risk / reward. Code fills the numbers; you write the words.

## 8. Predict, then react (13.7, "Predict, Then React")
First predict: how should the stock have moved on what the print justified? Then react: if it did not move that
way, make a bet. The seven days after the print matter as much as print day; the market is still searching for a
price. Say plainly whether the move fits the print.

## 9. Reaction patterns (13.7, "Reaction Patterns Worth Trading")
Name the one that fits, or none. Use these exact names:
- **hedge-fund dump that bounces:** hedge funds owned it, it missed their high bar, the selling is overdone; it
  tends to bounce by T+7. Tell: crowded long, small miss, big drop.
- **short squeeze that rolls over:** a crowded short squeezes on the print and tends to roll over by T+7. Tell: big
  pop on a print that did not change the numbers.
- **under-reaction:** the stock is up 10% but the 12-month target is up 15-20%. Tell: target change above the move.
- **conservative-guide panic:** investors panic at a cautious guide, then calm down. Tell: a guide below the Street
  from a company that habitually guides low and raises.
- **sustainable deceleration:** the business is really rolling over and the first leg down may not be the last
  ("shoot it in the back"). Tell: the key driver slowing, not one line missing.
- **weird reaction:** see section 10.
- **none:** the move fits the print.

## 10. Weird reactions (13.7, "Weird Reactions")
Positioning creates most weirdness: books unwinding, longs dumping, shorts squeezing. Many large holders use the
print as **exit liquidity**; one seller taking 5-10% of the day's volume can be the whole story. Confusion creates
a clarification trade, as management explains at conferences and the sell-side comes around. Apply humility: check
the historical and implied moves before calling a reaction wrong.

## 11. Fading moves (13.7, "Fading Moves and the Prospective IRR")
A sell-off on a bad quarter raises the IRR to an unchanged long-term value (context.md's fade table). The key is
telling apart what changes the **long-term value trajectory** from a temporary overreaction. Say which this is.

## 12. "Are we getting too cute?" (13.6)
Selling a stock you want to own later, to dodge one weak quarter, often misses the move you worked to find. It is a
batting-average game.

## 13. Judgment calls
List every judgment call you made. A call the user never hears about is the worst outcome.
